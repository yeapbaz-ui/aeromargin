AeroMargin

A Mars walking map that knows how much oxygen the way home costs.

Radio takes 4 to 24 minutes to reach Earth, one way. An astronaut on a surface walk cannot wait for Mission Control to say turn back.

AeroMargin joins orbital terrain data with a spacesuit oxygen model and draws one line on the helmet visor: the edge of the area you can still walk home from.

Background: layered ground in the Valles Marineris region, HiRISE, NASA/JPL-Caltech/UArizona

Live demo · Habitat console and visor HUD

Plan a walk. Watch the margin.

Choose a start A and a destination B, by tap, from the lists or by voice. The map marks ground as green, yellow or red, builds the safest route around the hazards and tells you what the walk costs in distance, time, steps and oxygen. The green contour is the point of no return: every place you can walk to from where you stand and still reach the airlock before the reserve.

      EVA log

        The terrain in this demo is generated in the browser. It is shaped after landforms seen in Melas Chasma (canyon wall, layered mound, dark dunes, block field, fault troughs) but it is not real elevation data. The oxygen model, the thresholds and the point-of-no-return rule are the ones in our specification. MERJ is science gain per litre of oxygen, ×1000. Science targets are numbered S1 to S5; the letters A and B mark the start and the destination of the route. Two hazard reports are preloaded as samples and can be removed. Pinch, or use the buttons on the map, to zoom; drag to move a zoomed map.

        The problem

        Today’s Mars maps show the ground, not the walk.

        • 4–24 minone-way radio delay between Mars and Earth
        • 0.38 gMars gravity; suit and astronaut weigh 725.6 N together
        • 0.38 → 2.4+ L/minoxygen use from standing still to climbing loose sand
        • 6 data layersoptical, terrain, thermal, geology, atmosphere, radiation

        No one to call

        With a 4 to 24 minute one-way delay, voice guidance from Earth arrives too late. The suit has to make the turn-back call on its own.

        Passive layers

        Portals such as NASA Mars Trek and the USGS mapping hub show relief and geology. They do not know how fast a person burns oxygen on that ground.

        Uphill is not downhill

        A slope that was easy on the way out is a climb on the way back. Loose sand can raise the energy cost of a step 2.5 to 3.4 times.

        Tired people push on

        NASA BASALT field studies link fatigue and rising CO₂ with fixation on the goal: just 40 more metres for the sample. The safe zone must shrink when the astronaut is tired.

        Where we land the project

        Valles Marineris, Melas Chasma

        The largest known canyon system in the Solar System runs along the Martian equator east of Tharsis and spans about a fifth of the planet’s circumference. It formed mainly by stretching and faulting of the crust; water reshaped it later.

        Why Melas Chasma

        • It is the widest part of the canyon and is densely covered by HiRISE images at about 25 cm per pixel.
        • Hydrated sulfates, a sign of past water, are found across the canyon and especially here.
        • Canyon walls collapse: one landslide is up to ~100 km long. The floor carries layered deposits whose origin is still an open question.
        • Eastern Melas has already been proposed as a human exploration zone: water, science targets within ~100 km, and moderate wind away from the walls.
        • A good zone needs water for life support, strong science targets, varied sites within ~100 km and a flat landing area of at least 5 × 5 km.

        How it works

        From terrain to litres of oxygen

        Simple trackers divide tank volume by a constant. Real oxygen use changes about 3.5 times with slope and soil, so we price every step.

        Try the model

        Oxygen flow against slope. Move the sliders and watch the gold point.

        Metabolic power of each step

        Suited mass is 195 kg (80 kg astronaut, 115 kg suit and life support pack). Standing in a pressurised suit costs 115 W. Climbing costs far more than walking flat; gentle descent gives a little back; soft ground adds a sink term scaled by thermal inertia.

        Bedrock I = 452 TIU, average regolith ~295, loose wind-blown sand under 180.

        Wmet = 115 + 0.28·m·g·v + 1.95·m·g·v·max(0, sinφ)
          − 0.45·m·g·v·max(0, −sinφ) + 38·(452 / Icell)1.65
        m·g = 195 kg × 3.721 m/s² = 725.6 N

        Watts to oxygen

        One standard litre of oxygen releases 20.2 kJ. At 280 W the suit uses 0.866 L/min. Tank pressure falls about 4.38 psia per litre.

        The time-left readout never divides by the instant flow. It uses a 3-minute moving average, so the clock does not jump from 2 hours to 7 when the astronaut stops to rest.

        VO2 = 0.00297·Wmet + 0.035  L/min
        Tleft = (Vnow − Vreserve) / VEMA, 180 s
        VEMA,t = α·Vt + (1 − α)·VEMA,t−1,  α = 1/180

        Point of no return

        Every 5 seconds the suit runs Dijkstra’s algorithm backwards from the airlock over the whole grid, with slopes reversed for the return leg. A cell is inside the survival zone only if the oxygen to walk home fits in what is left above the reserve.

        Fatigue F combines heart rate, heart-rate variability and helmet CO₂. At F = 0.65 the factor β drops from 1.00 to 0.74, holding back 26% more oxygen for a slower, clumsier walk home.

        Ereturn(x → airlock) ≤ βt·(Vnow − Vreserve)
        βt = 1 / (1 + 0.55·Ft)
        Ft = clip(0.40·HR term + 0.35·HRV term + 0.25·pCO2 / 6.0, 0, 1)

        The safest route, not the shortest

        Every cell gets a safety class. Red is closed: slopes over 30°, restricted RSL slopes and red zones reported by the crew. Yellow is caution: loose sand under 200 TIU, slopes over 18°, reported caution zones and a two-cell buffer around red zones. Everything else is green.

        The safest route runs the same search with oxygen cost multiplied on yellow ground, so it goes around hazards even when that is longer. The shortest option stays one tap away. Steps are route length divided by a 0.6 m suited stride.

        costsafe(cell) = EO2(cell) · kclass
        k = 1 green · 4 caution terrain · 3 buffer or level 1 report · 8 level 2 report
        red cells: never entered
        steps ≈ route length / 0.6 m

        Science per litre

        Each target carries a geological uncertainty. Sampling one collapses the uncertainty at similar sites, and the suit re-ranks what is left by information gained per litre of oxygen, discounted where radiation is higher. Canyon walls block part of the sky and lower the dose.

        ΔI(x) = 0.5·ln(1 + σ²(x) / σ²noise)
        MERJ(x) = ΔI(x) / (EO2(x)·(1 + λrad·D(x)))
        D(x) = DRAD·Ψsky(x),  Ψsky ∈ [0.45, 1.0]

        One packet per second

        Suit, tablet and visor share a SuitTelemetryPacket at 1 Hz, in the style used by NASA SUITS student teams. This one is written by the demo above and changes as you walk.

        What this is and is not
        
    
        Honest limits
        
  
        
  
        
    
          
      
        • The demo terrain is synthetic. Real MOLA, HRSC and CTX elevation tiles for Melas Chasma are the next step.
          • 
      
        • Soil and slope coefficients were set from Gale crater data (Curiosity, REMS thermal inertia, Mount Sharp traverse), not from the canyon.
          • 
      
        • Radiation figures come from Gale crater near solar maximum. Treat them as an estimate for Mars in general.
          • 
      
        • Whether the thicker air column on the canyon floor shields better than the plateau is a hypothesis. No one has measured it inside the canyon.
          • 
    
        
    
          
      
        • No rover has worked inside Valles Marineris, so every photograph here is taken from orbit. First-person frames would come from Curiosity and Perseverance and be labelled as analogues.
          • 
      
        • Walking speed of 0.8 m/s, the braking penalty on descents steeper than 12°, the radiation weight in MERJ and the target uncertainties are demo assumptions.
          • 
      
        • Canyon dimensions differ between sources (3,000–4,000 km long, 7–9 km deep).
          • 
      
        • Hazard reports are saved in the browser and shared between open tabs. Sharing between devices needs a database address in AEROMARGIN_DB_URL; without it each device keeps its own copy.
          • 
      
        • Sound cues, the theme and the call sign are saved in this browser only. The EVA report is a file you download; nothing is sent anywhere.
          • 
      
        • Voice input uses the browser’s speech recognition (Chrome, Edge, Safari). Every command can also be typed. The AR view is a preview in the browser, not a headset build.
          • 
      
        • Terrain photos are orbital HiRISE images of the same kind of landform, not pictures of the exact spot. The 0.6 m step length and the route penalty factors are demo assumptions.
          • 
      
        • Planned build: Python and GDAL for data, a TypeScript or Rust core for routing in under 15 ms per frame, React with MapLibre and WebXR for tablet and visor.
